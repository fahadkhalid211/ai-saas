P: Coaching is personal. Prospective clients want to know whether a coach understands their situation, whether the chemistry will work and whether the investment makes sense, and coaches want to work with people who are ready to change. Enquiries come through websites, referrals and events, and the first conversation is usually a 'chemistry call'. Too many of those calls are with people who are curious but not ready, or who cannot afford the work, and the coach is left giving free advice. A chat can help with the early part: explain the approach, answer common questions, share the structure and fees, ask a few questions that show readiness, and book chemistry calls with people who are a good match. It should do so warmly, because a cold, transactional first impression is a poor start for a relationship business. This guide explains how.
H2[what-prospective-clients-ask]: What prospective clients ask
TABLE:
H: Question | What to say
R: What is coaching and how is it different from therapy or consulting? | Your plain-language definition and boundaries
R: What is your approach and experience? | Your method, training and the kinds of clients you serve
R: How does it work? | Session length, frequency, duration and format
R: How much does it cost? | Your fee structure, or a range and what it includes
R: Who pays: me or my employer? | How you work with sponsors, and how confidentiality is handled
R: Can I try a session? | Your chemistry call or trial arrangement
R: Is it confidential? | Your confidentiality commitments and their limits
H2[boundaries-coaching-is-not-therapy]: Boundaries: coaching is not therapy
UL:
- The assistant should not diagnose or offer mental health advice.
- If a visitor describes distress, crisis or thoughts of self-harm, it should respond with care, share appropriate support resources and offer to connect them with a person.
- It should explain that coaching is future-focused and that it may not be suitable for certain needs.
- It should not promise outcomes.
P: Write these lines with care and test them with sensitive messages. A caring, clear response matters more than any sales flow.
H2[readiness-questions-that-feel-natural]: Readiness questions that feel natural
TABLE:
H: Question | Wording | What it shows
R: What prompted you to look for a coach now? | What is happening that made now the right time? | Motivation and urgency
R: What would success look like? | If we worked together for six months, what would be different? | Clarity of goals
R: Have you worked with a coach before? | Have you had coaching or mentoring before? | Experience and expectations
R: Who is funding it? | Is this something you are arranging yourself, or through your organisation? | Sponsor process
R: What is your timeline? | When would you like to start? | Availability fit
P: Three or four questions are enough. Avoid probing personal history in a chat; that belongs in the conversation with you.
H2[self-funded-and-sponsored-clients]: Self-funded and sponsored clients
TABLE:
H: Client | Process
R: Self-funded | Explain fees and terms clearly; offer a chemistry call
R: Employer-sponsored | Explain how sponsor agreements work: who signs, who receives reports and what stays confidential; capture the sponsor's contact if appropriate
R: HR-led programme | Route to your programme team or a proposal conversation
P: Sponsored engagements involve a third party, so clarity about confidentiality is essential. The assistant should not promise anything about what will be shared; it should say that terms are agreed in writing and explained on the call.
H2[book-the-chemistry-call]: Book the chemistry call
OL:
- Explain what it is: length, format and cost, usually free.
- Set expectations: this is a conversation to see whether you are a match, not a coaching session.
- Offer real times, in the visitor's time zone if needed.
- Confirm by email with the video link and a short pre-call question or two.
- Send a reminder and a change link.

H2[fees-and-value]: Fees and value
P: Coaching is a considered purchase. Being open about fees respects the visitor and filters enquiries. State your packages in plain terms: what is included, the duration and the investment. If you offer sliding scales or pro bono places, describe how they work. Avoid pressure tactics, false scarcity or promises of transformation.
H2[confidentiality-and-data]: Confidentiality and data
UL:
- Tell visitors the chat is an AI assistant and link to your privacy policy.
- Ask them not to share sensitive personal information in the chat.
- Collect only what you need to book: name, contact and a line about what they are looking for.
- Keep transcripts secure and delete them when no longer needed.
- Follow the code of ethics of your coaching body, and the privacy law that applies to you.

H2[a-sample-conversation]: A sample conversation
TABLE:
H: Speaker | Message
R: Visitor | I have just been promoted to director and I feel out of my depth. Do you coach new leaders?
R: Assistant | Congratulations, and it is very common to feel that way at first. Yes, we work with newly promoted leaders, usually over six months with sessions every two weeks. I am an AI assistant, and Priya would be your coach. Would you like a free 30-minute chemistry call to see if it feels right? What has been the hardest part so far?
R: Visitor | Managing people who used to be my peers.
R: Assistant | That is a classic challenge, and one Priya works on a lot. I can offer Wednesday at 5 p.m. or Friday at 9 a.m. What name and email should I use?
H2[follow-up-and-nurture]: Follow-up and nurture
UL:
- After the call, send a thank-you with next steps and no pressure.
- For those not ready, offer a resource and permission to check in later.
- Ask for feedback on the process, to improve it.
- Invite clients who complete their programme to share testimonials, only with consent.

H2[a-worked-example-a-leadership-coach]: A worked example: a leadership coach
P: A leadership coach was getting two chemistry calls a week, many with people who wanted free advice or who were looking for therapy. She added a chat that explained her approach and boundaries, stated her package range and asked two readiness questions. Visitors seeking therapy were redirected kindly to appropriate resources, and those interested in coaching booked calls. Her chemistry calls fell to one or two a week, and more became clients. The chat also gave her a record of what people were asking for, which she used to write two new articles that brought in more of the right clients. She noted that the greatest benefit was calm: every call began with a person who understood what she offered.
H2[common-mistakes]: Common mistakes
UL:
- Turning a warm chat into a sales funnel.
- Answering personal distress with a booking prompt.
- Promising transformation.
- Hiding fees.
- Not explaining sponsor confidentiality clearly.
- Booking calls without time zone clarity.
H2[a-short-pre-launch-checklist]: A short pre-launch checklist
UL:
- Your approach, boundaries and confidentiality terms are written in your own voice.
- Sensitive-message rules are tested: distress, crisis and requests for therapy.
- Fees and packages are stated plainly.
- The chemistry call is described honestly.
- Alerts and booking work, and time zones are clear.
H2[frequently-asked-questions]: Frequently asked questions
FAQ:
Q: Can a chatbot qualify coaching clients?
A: Yes, with a few warm questions about motivation, goals and timing, and it can book chemistry calls.
Q: Should it discuss personal problems?
A: No. It should keep to booking and general information, respond with care to distress and offer a person and appropriate resources.
Q: How do I handle employer-sponsored coaching?
A: Explain that terms are agreed in writing and that confidentiality will be explained on the call, and route to the right person.
Q: Should I show my fees?
A: Being clear about fees respects visitors and filters enquiries.
Q: What about confidentiality?
A: Ask visitors not to share sensitive details in the chat, and keep transcripts secure.
Q: Is a chemistry call free?
A: Usually, but say exactly what it includes and its length.
