P: Cost per lead is a popular number, but it can mislead: ten cheap enquiries that never book are worse than three expensive ones that do. For appointment-based businesses, the number that matters more is the cost of a booked appointment, and beyond it the cost of an attended one. It ties your marketing, software and staff spending to the outcome that actually pays. This guide shows how to calculate it, what to include, worked examples, how to break it down by source and how to bring it down, including with an AI assistant that captures more bookings from the traffic you already pay for.
H2[the-basic-formula]: The basic formula
P: Cost per booked appointment = total cost of acquiring bookings in a period ÷ number of appointments booked in that period.
TABLE:
H: Term | Meaning
R: Total cost | Marketing spend plus tools plus the staff time spent handling enquiries and booking
R: Booked appointments | Confirmed appointments created in the period, from all sources you count
R: Period | Usually a month; longer for seasonal businesses
P: Use the same definition every month so you can compare. If you want a stricter measure, divide by attended appointments, which accounts for no-shows.
H2[what-to-include-in-cost]: What to include in cost
TABLE:
H: Cost | Examples | Include?
R: Advertising | Search ads, social ads, sponsored listings | Yes
R: Agency or freelancer fees | SEO, ad management, content | Yes, for the work that drives bookings
R: Software | Website, booking tool, chat assistant, AI usage, CRM | Yes, the share that supports acquisition and booking
R: Staff time | Time answering enquiries, calls, scheduling | Yes, priced at their hourly cost
R: Discounts and offers | Introductory pricing | Yes if they exist to win bookings
R: Overheads unrelated to acquisition | Rent, utilities | No
P: Start simple: advertising, tools and estimated staff time. You can refine later.

H2[how-to-lower-cost-per-booking]: How to lower cost per booking
OL:
- **Convert more of the traffic you have.** Clear offers, fast answers and easy booking raise bookings without raising spend. See [the service business CRO playbook](/blog/service-business-cro-playbook).
- **Capture out-of-hours enquiries.** A chat that answers and books at any hour recovers enquiries that would otherwise be lost. See [after-hours appointment booking](/blog/after-hours-appointment-booking).
- **Reply faster.** Speed to lead lifts conversion; see [speed to lead](/blog/speed-to-lead-service-businesses).
- **Reduce staff time.** Automate routine questions so staff time per booking falls.
- **Cut waste in ads.** Pause campaigns with high cost per booking, and shift budget to sources that book.
- **Reduce no-shows,** so booked appointments become attended ones.
P: Each lever changes the numerator or the denominator: spending less, or booking more.

H2[pitfalls]: Pitfalls
UL:
- Ignoring staff time, which is often the largest cost.
- Counting only paid channels and forgetting that organic bookings still cost effort.
- Mixing periods, so one month's spend is compared with another month's bookings.
- Ignoring no-shows and cancellations.
- Over-attributing every booking to the last click.
- Chasing the lowest number at the expense of customer quality.
H2[a-step-by-step-way-to-set-up-your-calculation]: A step-by-step way to set up your calculation
OL:
- Choose the period: the last full month, or the last three for seasonal businesses.
- List every advertising and agency cost for that period.
- List every tool that supports getting bookings, and take the monthly cost of each.
- Estimate the hours staff spent answering enquiries and scheduling, and multiply by their hourly cost including employer costs.
- Add them up to get total cost.
- Count appointments booked from all sources in the same period.
- Divide total cost by appointments booked.
- Repeat for each source where you can separate the costs and bookings.
P: Put the results in a sheet with one row per month. Within three months you will see a trend, and within six you will see seasonality.
H2[cost-per-booked-appointment-and-lifetime-value]: Cost per booked appointment and lifetime value
P: A low cost per booking is good, but it means little without knowing what a customer is worth. If a customer books once and never returns, the first appointment must pay back the acquisition cost. If a customer returns eight times a year, you can afford to pay much more to win them. Compare cost per booked appointment with the average gross profit per customer over a sensible period, such as a year. The ratio tells you whether growth is affordable.
TABLE:
H: Business | Cost per booking | Customer value over a year | Comfortable ratio
R: Barbershop | $7 | $600 (regular monthly cuts) | Very comfortable
R: Dental practice | $45 | $700 | Comfortable
R: Home repair | $80 per estimate | $450 per job, occasional repeat | Depends on win rate and margin
R: Consultant | $120 per call | $4,000 | Comfortable if close rate holds
P: These examples are illustrative, but the logic is general: what you can afford to pay depends on what a customer is worth.
H2[a-monthly-review-routine]: A monthly review routine
P: Once a month, update your sheet and ask three questions. Which source had the lowest cost per booking, and can we do more of it? Which had the highest, and is it still worth it? What changed in our process this month, and did the number move? Then choose one action for the next month, such as pausing an underperforming ad group or improving the booking page. One change at a time lets you learn what works.
H2[a-quick-sensitivity-check]: A quick sensitivity check
P: Before you make a decision from your numbers, test how sensitive it is. If cost per booking is $20 and you assumed staff time cost $15 an hour, what if it is really $25? If ten percent of bookings were duplicates, how much does the cost rise? If marketing spend is double next month, how many extra bookings do you need to keep the same cost? Working through three what-if cases takes ten minutes and tells you whether a conclusion is solid or fragile.
H2[putting-the-number-to-work]: Putting the number to work
P: A number only helps if it changes a decision. Decide in advance what you will do at different levels: if cost per booking rises above a threshold for two months, review the highest-cost source; if it falls below a comfortable level, consider spending more to grow; if it is stable, focus on reducing no-shows. Writing the rule down before you see the number prevents wishful thinking, and it turns the calculation from a report into a management tool.
H2[frequently-asked-questions]: Frequently asked questions
FAQ:
Q: What is cost per booked appointment?
A: Total acquisition costs in a period divided by the number of appointments booked in that period.
Q: How is it different from cost per lead?
A: A lead may never book. Cost per booked appointment measures the outcome that generates revenue.
Q: What costs should I include?
A: Advertising, agency fees, relevant software and the staff time spent handling enquiries and bookings.
Q: How do I lower it?
A: Convert more of your traffic, answer faster, capture out-of-hours enquiries, reduce staff time on routine questions and cut wasted spend.
Q: Should I use attended or booked appointments?
A: Track both. Attended appointments account for no-shows and give a truer picture of cost.
Q: Does a chatbot lower cost per booking?
A: It can, if it recovers bookings and saves staff time by more than it costs. Measure before and after.
