P: Service businesses that quote by the job, such as cleaners, painters, landscapers, movers and consultants, often face the same website question: should we add a price calculator, or a chat assistant that talks to the visitor and collects their details? Both promise the same outcome, more enquiries with less phone tag. They work quite differently, attract different kinds of visitor and produce leads of different quality. A calculator gives an instant number and can feel efficient, but it also asks the visitor to trust a simplified formula and often ends in a form. A chat assistant can answer questions, explain what affects the price and ask for details in context, but it will not produce an exact quote unless you have decided how. This guide compares the two honestly, explains the strengths and weaknesses of each, shows when each fits, suggests a combined approach and provides a simple way to test them on your own website without guessing.
H2[how-each-one-works]: How each one works
TABLE:
H:  | Cost estimator | Conversational lead capture
R: What the visitor does | Selects options and sees a price or range | Asks questions in their own words and answers questions from the assistant
R: What you get | A number plus the choices behind it, often a form | A conversation, plus name and contact details
R: Effort for the visitor | Low if the options are clear | Low; typing or tapping suggested questions
R: Setup effort | Design the formula and options | Write documents describing services and pricing rules
R: Best at | Standardised jobs with predictable pricing | Explaining, reassuring and gathering context
H2[strengths-of-calculators]: Strengths of calculators
UL:
- Instant answer to 'roughly how much?', which is the top question for many visitors.
- Structured data: the leads arrive with sizes, options and postcodes already filled in.
- Filters out visitors whose budget is far below your minimum.
- Works well for standardised services, such as a clean by number of rooms.
H2[weaknesses-of-calculators]: Weaknesses of calculators
UL:
- Real jobs rarely fit the formula, so the estimate can mislead.
- Visitors may treat the number as a firm quote and be upset later.
- There is no place to ask 'does that include the oven?'; questions remain unanswered.
- A long form before the price loses people; a price before contact details loses the lead.
- They need upkeep as prices change.
H2[strengths-of-conversational-capture]: Strengths of conversational capture
UL:
- Answers questions that a calculator cannot, such as what is included and what affects the price.
- Feels personal and works well on phones.
- Captures details naturally as the conversation goes, and can ask follow-up questions.
- Handles visitors with unusual jobs.
- Works out of hours and links each lead to the conversation that produced it.
H2[weaknesses-of-conversational-capture]: Weaknesses of conversational capture
UL:
- It gives ranges and rules, not an exact calculation, unless you set out the formula in documents.
- Quality depends on the documents; if pricing is missing, the assistant cannot answer.
- Some visitors prefer to tap through options without reading.
- Leads are less structured, although fields you choose can be collected.
P: Crescelo's lead capture asks for details you choose, such as service, budget or preferred time, and sends each lead by email, to Slack, to Google Sheets or to a webhook, linked to the chat that produced it.
H2[lead-quality-what-to-expect]: Lead quality: what to expect
TABLE:
H: Aspect | Calculator leads | Chat leads
R: Volume | Often lower, because it asks more upfront | Often higher, because it asks less at the start
R: Structure | Very structured | Structured for the fields you set, plus a transcript
R: Fit with your prices | Filtered by the estimate | Filtered by how clearly the assistant states price ranges
R: Context | Options chosen | The visitor's own words and worries
R: Follow-up effort | Low for standard jobs | Depends; the transcript helps you prepare
P: These are tendencies, not laws, and they vary by industry. The only reliable answer for your business comes from testing.
H2[when-to-use-which]: When to use which
OL:
- **Standardised, repeatable jobs** with few variables: a calculator can work well, ideally with the assistant available to answer questions.
- **Custom or variable jobs:** a chat that explains the price factors and gathers details for a real quote.
- **High-ticket services:** chat, because trust and reassurance matter more than a number.
- **Very price-sensitive markets:** a calculator, or a clear starting-price statement plus chat.
- **Small teams with little time:** chat that filters and prepares leads, so quotes are faster.
H2[the-combined-approach]: The combined approach
P: You do not have to choose. A common pattern is to publish clear starting prices or ranges on your service pages, offer a simple estimator only if your jobs are standardised, and place the assistant beside it to answer questions and capture the details. The assistant explains what the estimate includes and what could change it; the calculator or price document supplies the numbers. Make it clear that any figure is an estimate and how the final quote is confirmed.
UL:
- Publish ranges and what changes them, in the knowledge base as well as on the page.
- Have the assistant ask the two or three questions that most affect price: size, location and timing.
- Offer a callback or a visit for an exact quote.
- Never let the assistant promise a fixed price it is not authorised to give.
H2[how-to-test-them-fairly]: How to test them fairly
TABLE:
H: Step | How
R: Define the goal | Qualified enquiries per hundred visitors, and share that turn into customers
R: Run each for a fixed period | Two to four weeks each, in similar seasons, or split traffic if your tools allow
R: Count the same things | Leads, qualified leads, quotes sent, jobs won, time spent per lead
R: Read samples | Look at ten leads from each and judge their quality
R: Decide | Keep the approach that produces more won jobs per hour of your time, not the most leads
P: Beware of counting raw lead volume; a tool that produces many poor leads can cost more time than it saves. See [measuring chat results](/blog/service-business-chat-analytics-playbook).
H2[data-and-privacy]: Data and privacy
P: Both approaches collect personal data. Say why you need it, collect only what you use and follow the rules for your area. See [privacy law for lead capture](/blog/chatbot-lead-capture-gdpr-ccpa-compliance).
H2[common-mistakes]: Common mistakes
UL:
- Presenting an estimate as a firm quote.
- Asking for contact details before showing any price information.
- Forgetting to update the formula when prices change.
- Judging by lead volume alone.
- Assuming visitors will read long instructions.
- Not connecting leads to a follow-up process.
H2[frequently-asked-questions]: Frequently asked questions
FAQ:
Q: Are price calculators better than chatbots for leads?
A: Calculators suit standardised jobs; chat suits variable or high-value work and answers questions. Many businesses use both.
Q: Can a chat assistant give a price?
A: It can state prices and ranges from your documents. An exact quote for a custom job usually needs a person.
Q: Which produces better leads?
A: It depends on your services. Test both and compare won jobs per hour of your time.
Q: How do I stop visitors treating an estimate as a quote?
A: Label it clearly as an estimate and explain what could change it and how the final quote is confirmed.
Q: Can Crescelo send leads to my tools?
A: Yes: email alerts, Slack, Google Sheets and signed webhooks for Zapier, Make and similar tools. Native CRM sync is planned.
Q: Do I need both?
A: Not necessarily. Start with clear price information and a chat assistant, and add a calculator only if your jobs are standardised.
