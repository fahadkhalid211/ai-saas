P: Designing when the AI hands over is half of a good handover. The other half is what your team does when they arrive. A visitor who has been chatting with an assistant and then meets a person is in a delicate moment. If the person says 'How can I help?' as though the conversation never happened, the visitor feels ignored. If the person is slow, curt or contradicts what the assistant said, trust drops. If the person reads the history, greets them by name, picks up the thread and solves the problem, the visitor remembers the business as helpful and organised. This guide is for the people who take over: what to check before you type, how to greet, how to handle mistakes made by the assistant, how to keep the right tone, how fast to respond, how to close the loop and how to train and review your team.
H2[before-you-type-read-the-conversation]: Before you type: read the conversation
OL:
- Read the whole conversation, not just the last message.
- Note the visitor's name, the problem in their own words and what they already tried.
- Check what the assistant told them: prices, times, promises.
- Note the reason for the handover: request, complaint, sensitive topic or unanswerable question.
- Check any contact details captured.
- Decide what you can do and what you need to find out.
P: Thirty seconds of reading prevents most of the mistakes that make handovers feel bad.
H2[greeting-that-shows-you-have-read-it]: Greeting that shows you have read it
TABLE:
H: Weak | Better
R: Hi, how can I help? | Hi Sam, this is Priya from the team. I have read your conversation with our assistant, and I understand you want to move your Thursday appointment and you were charged twice. Let me look at both.
R: Hello. | Hi Sam, thank you for your patience. I am Priya and I have your chat in front of me.
R: What is your problem? | I am sorry about the double charge. Let me check that now.
UL:
- Use their name.
- Say who you are.
- Show that you know the situation.
- Acknowledge feelings when relevant, briefly and sincerely.
H2[speed-and-expectations]: Speed and expectations
P: Handover is a promise: a person is coming. Keep it. Reply within the time the assistant stated, and if you cannot solve the problem immediately, say what you will do and when. A quick 'I am checking this now and will reply in ten minutes' is far better than a long silence. If you are the wrong person, say who will take over and pass the conversation on properly.
H2[correct-the-assistant-without-blaming-it]: Correct the assistant without blaming it
P: The assistant may have said something wrong or incomplete. Correct it plainly: 'I need to correct something. The price I see for that service is $95, not $85.' Do not blame the software or the customer, and do not make a fuss. Then record the error so the knowledge base can be fixed. See [how to review your chatbot's conversations each week](/blog/audit-website-chatbot-conversations).
H2[tone]: Tone
UL:
- Warm and plain, like a good receptionist.
- Short paragraphs; avoid jargon.
- Match the customer's level of formality, within your brand.
- Do not over-apologise, but do own real mistakes.
- Never argue; stay calm when someone is angry.
- Avoid canned phrases that ignore what the customer said.
P: See [de-escalating frustrated customers with AI guardrails](/blog/de-escalating-frustrated-customers-ai-guardrails).
H2[privacy-and-sensitive-information]: Privacy and sensitive information
UL:
- Do not ask for sensitive details in an open chat; move to a secure route.
- Verify identity before discussing account details, where relevant.
- Do not copy the conversation into unsecured places.
- Follow your privacy policy for how long chats are kept.
H2[closing-the-loop]: Closing the loop
OL:
- Confirm the outcome: what was done and what happens next.
- Ask whether there is anything else.
- Record the outcome in your system: resolved, booked, follow-up needed.
- If a follow-up is promised, put it in your task list, with a time.
- Note anything the assistant should learn for the future.
P: A handover that ends with 'thanks, bye' and no record leaves the next person without context.
H2[hand-back-to-the-assistant-or-not]: Hand back to the assistant, or not
P: Decide with your team whether the conversation returns to the assistant after you finish, or ends. If it returns, say so: 'I will let the assistant help you with booking, and I am still here if you need me.' If you take over, keep control until the end so the visitor does not hear two voices.
H2[handling-out-of-hours-takeovers]: Handling out-of-hours takeovers
P: When a handover arrives outside your hours, the first person in the next morning should start with an apology for the wait if the assistant promised a time, and with the conversation history in front of them. Prioritise by urgency: emergencies first, then complaints, then routine requests. See [live human handover flow design](/blog/live-human-handover-flow-design) for how to design the process around it.
H2[training-your-team]: Training your team
UL:
- Practise with three real past conversations: read, greet, resolve, close.
- Agree a standard for greeting and response times.
- Share examples of good handovers and bad ones.
- Make it easy to record assistant errors.
- Review a sample of handovers each week for the first month, then monthly.
TABLE:
H: Skill | How to practise
R: Reading fast | Time yourself on past conversations
R: Tone | Read replies aloud; ask a colleague to review
R: Correcting the assistant | Role-play awkward corrections
R: Difficult conversations | Role-play a complaint

TABLE:
H: Speaker | Message
R: Assistant | I am passing you to a team member now. They can see our conversation.
R: Team member | Hi Sam, this is Priya. I have read your chat about the appointment on Thursday and the double charge, and I am sorry for the trouble. I can see two payments of $60 on the 4th. TABLE:

H2[a-one-page-handover-checklist]: A one-page handover checklist
UL:
- Read the full conversation.
- Greet by name and say who you are.
- Show what you have understood.
- Correct any mistake the assistant made, calmly.
- Say what you will do and by when.
- Do it, and confirm the outcome.
- Record the result and any content fix needed.
- Ask whether there is anything else.
P: Print this and keep it beside the screen for the first month. Once the routine is habit, the handover feels natural and the customer barely notices the join between assistant and person.
H2[common-mistakes]: Common mistakes
UL:
- Starting with 'How can I help?' as if nothing was said.
- Contradicting the assistant without explanation.
- Leaving the visitor waiting with no update.
- Asking for information already given.
- Forgetting to record the outcome.
H2[when-the-visitor-has-left]: When the visitor has left
P: Sometimes a visitor closes the chat before you arrive. Use the contact details the assistant captured to reach them, by the method they chose, and refer to the conversation: 'You asked our assistant about moving your appointment; I have done that for you.' If no contact details exist, record the case so you can respond if they return.
H2[frequently-asked-questions]: Frequently asked questions
FAQ:
Q: What should I do first when I take over a chat?
A: Read the whole conversation, note what the visitor wants and what the assistant said, then greet them by name and show you have read it.
Q: How fast should I respond?
A: Within the time the assistant promised, or sooner. If you need longer, say when you will reply.
Q: What if the assistant gave the wrong answer?
A: Correct it plainly without blaming anyone, and record it so the knowledge base can be fixed.
Q: Should the assistant continue after I join?
A: Usually not. Keep control until the end to avoid two voices, unless you deliberately hand back.
Q: How do I train my team?
A: Practise with real past conversations, agree standards for greetings and response times and review samples regularly.
