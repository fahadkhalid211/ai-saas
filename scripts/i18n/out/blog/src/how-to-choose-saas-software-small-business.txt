P: Choosing business software feels like it should be easy: search, compare a few feature lists, pick the one with the most ticks. In practice it is one of the most expensive small decisions a business makes, because the real cost is not the subscription. It is the hours spent setting it up, the data you put into it, the habits your team builds around it and the pain of leaving later. Many small businesses end up paying for three overlapping tools that nobody fully uses. This guide gives you a repeatable process for choosing software that fits: define the problem before you look at products, separate must-haves from nice-to-haves, build a short list, test with real work, check security and total cost, and plan your exit before you sign up. It works for a CRM, an accounting tool, a scheduling system, a chatbot or anything else you pay for monthly.
H2[step-1-write-down-the-problem-not-the-product]: Step 1: Write down the problem, not the product
P: Start with one or two sentences describing what is going wrong today. 'We lose about a third of enquiries because nobody replies out of hours' is a problem. 'We need a chatbot' is a solution you have already chosen. Writing the problem first keeps you from buying features you will never use, and it gives you a way to judge success later.
TABLE:
H: Vague request | Better problem statement
R: We need a CRM | Leads are tracked in three spreadsheets and follow-ups are forgotten
R: We need scheduling software | Customers phone to book and we double-book about twice a month
R: We need an AI assistant | The same ten questions take two hours of staff time each day
R: We need accounting software | Invoicing takes a full day each month and payments are chased manually
H2[step-2-set-must-haves-nice-to-haves-and-deal-breakers]: Step 2: Set must-haves, nice-to-haves and deal-breakers
UL:
- **Must-haves:** without these the tool does not solve the problem. Keep to five or fewer.
- **Nice-to-haves:** helpful but you could live without them.
- **Deal-breakers:** for example, data stored outside your region, no export, no two-factor sign-in or a price that grows unpredictably.
P: This list is your scorecard. Products that fail a must-have are out, however impressive their marketing pages look.
H2[step-3-ask-who-else-needs-to-be-involved]: Step 3: Ask who else needs to be involved
P: Software is used by people. Before shortlisting, name who will use it daily, who will administer it and who pays. Ask the daily users what annoys them about the current way of working; their answers are often more useful than any feature list. Tools chosen by one person and imposed on a team frequently sit unused.
H2[step-4-build-a-shortlist-of-three]: Step 4: Build a shortlist of three
OL:
- Ask peers in your industry what they use and what they regret.
- Search for the problem, not the category, and read comparison pages with care since many are written by vendors.
- Check independent review sites, and read the three-star reviews, which are usually the most honest.
- Look at the vendor's own documentation and pricing page: clear, specific pages usually mean a mature product.
- Pick three. More than that and you will not test any properly.
H2[step-5-test-with-your-real-work]: Step 5: Test with your real work
P: A demo shows the product at its best. A trial with your own data shows how it behaves on an ordinary Tuesday. Give each shortlisted tool the same tasks:
TABLE:
H: Test | What it reveals
R: Set up from scratch without help | How much the onboarding really costs in time
R: Import a real spreadsheet or your documents | Whether your data fits and imports cleanly
R: Complete your three most common tasks | Whether daily use is quick or clumsy
R: Ask support a real question | Response time and quality before you are a customer
R: Let a colleague try it with no instructions | How intuitive it is
P: Set a time limit, such as a week, and score each tool against your must-have list afterwards while the experience is fresh.
H2[step-6-check-security-and-privacy]: Step 6: Check security and privacy
UL:
- Does it support two-factor authentication and role-based access for your team?
- Where is data stored, and who are the sub-processors?
- Can you export all of your data in a common format, and delete it on request?
- Is there a data processing agreement if you handle personal data?
- How are stored credentials and API keys protected?
- Does the vendor publish a security or privacy page with specifics?
P: Do not accept claims of certifications that you cannot verify; ask for evidence or check the vendor's public documents. For personal data, see the [GDPR checklist for website chatbots](/blog/website-chatbot-gdpr-checklist), and for team access, [securing your account with roles and two-factor authentication](/blog/setup-rbac-2fa-team-security).
H2[step-7-work-out-the-total-cost]: Step 7: Work out the total cost
TABLE:
H: Cost | Questions to ask
R: Subscription | Per user, per month, per feature? What happens when the team grows?
R: Usage charges | Are there limits on messages, contacts, storage or API calls, and what do overages cost?
R: Set-up time | Hours to configure, import data and train staff, multiplied by your hourly cost
R: Add-ons | Which of the features you need are in higher tiers?
R: Integrations | Do connections to other tools cost extra?
R: Exit | What does it cost in time to move away?
P: Where a product passes through a third-party cost, such as an AI provider, check whether the vendor adds a margin. Some offer a bring-your-own-key model with no markup; see [BYOK versus bundled pricing](/blog/byok-vs-bundled-ai-pricing-cost-comparison).
H2[step-8-plan-your-exit-before-you-enter]: Step 8: Plan your exit before you enter
UL:
- Confirm you can export your data in an open format such as CSV.
- Check the cancellation process and notice period.
- Prefer monthly billing until you are sure, even if annual is cheaper.
- Keep a copy of your key data outside the tool.
- Note how the contract handles price increases.
H2[step-9-decide-then-run-a-pilot]: Step 9: Decide, then run a pilot
P: Pick the tool with the best score against your must-haves, not the one you liked most in the demo. Roll it out to a small group first, define what success looks like, such as 'replies to enquiries within an hour' and check after a month. Write down what you learned; it makes the next software decision faster.
H2[common-mistakes]: Common mistakes
UL:
- Buying on features instead of the problem.
- Skipping a trial with real data.
- Ignoring the cost of staff time.
- Locking into an annual plan too early.
- Letting one enthusiast choose for everyone.
- Never reviewing whether the tool still earns its cost.
H2[frequently-asked-questions]: Frequently asked questions
FAQ:
Q: How do I choose the right software for my business?
A: Define the problem, list must-haves, shortlist three products, test them with real work, check security and total cost, and plan your exit.
Q: How many products should I compare?
A: Three is a good number: enough to compare, few enough to test properly.
Q: Should I choose the cheapest option?
A: Not necessarily. Include set-up time, limits and the cost of switching, which often exceed the subscription.
Q: What should I check about security?
A: Two-factor sign-in, roles, where data is stored, export and deletion, and how credentials are protected.
Q: Is an annual plan worth it?
A: Only after a successful pilot. Start monthly if you can.
Q: How often should I review my software?
A: Once or twice a year: cancel what is unused and check that what remains still solves the original problem.
