P: Reminders are the cheapest way to reduce no-shows, but the channel you choose changes how well they work. Text messages are noticed within minutes, while emails hold more detail and cost almost nothing. Most businesses do best with a combination, timed sensibly and worded like a person would write. This guide compares SMS and email for appointment reminders, explains timing, consent and wording, gives templates you can adapt and shows how to test what works for your customers.
NOTE: Rules on text messaging and email marketing differ by country. Reminders for an appointment the customer booked are generally treated differently from marketing, but you should check the laws that apply to you, such as consent requirements for text messages, and keep records of consent. This is general information, not legal advice.
H2[why-reminders-work]: Why reminders work
P: Most missed appointments are forgotten, not cancelled on purpose. A timely nudge, with an easy way to change the booking, turns forgetting into either attending or rescheduling. Both outcomes are better than an empty slot. The main choices are which channel, how many reminders and when to send them.
H2[sms-reminders-strengths-and-limits]: SMS reminders: strengths and limits
UL:
- **Fast attention.** Text messages are usually read within minutes, on the device the customer carries.
- **Short and direct.** A line with the time and a link is easy to act on.
- **Good for same-day reminders.** A message a few hours before catches people before they leave.
- **Two-way potential.** Customers can reply to confirm or ask to change, if you set that up.
P: Limits: they cost a small amount per message, length is limited, links must be trusted, some customers find texts intrusive and you need a valid mobile number and, in many places, appropriate consent.
H2[email-reminders-strengths-and-limits]: Email reminders: strengths and limits
UL:
- **Room for detail.** Address, map, parking, what to bring, preparation, price and policy all fit.
- **Almost free.** You can send several without cost concerns.
- **A record.** Customers can search for it later and add it to a calendar.
- **Calendar files.** A one-click 'add to calendar' reduces forgetting.
P: Limits: they are opened more slowly, may land in spam or be buried, and are less reliable for last-minute reminders.

H2[timing-that-works]: Timing that works
P: A pattern that suits many businesses is below. Adjust it to your service and how far ahead people book.
TABLE:
H: When | Channel | Purpose
R: Right after booking | Email (with calendar file) | Confirm details and share policies
R: 2 to 3 days before | Email or SMS | First reminder, with a change link
R: The day before | SMS | Short reminder, optional confirm
R: 2 to 3 hours before | SMS | Last reminder with address and change link
R: After the appointment | Email | Thanks, review request and rebooking link
P: For services booked weeks ahead, add a reminder a week before. For same-day bookings, send the confirmation and one short reminder. Three reminders in total is usually plenty; more tends to annoy.
H2[wording-that-gets-replies]: Wording that gets replies
TABLE:
H: Type | Weak | Stronger
R: Day-before reminder | Reminder: appointment tomorrow. | Hi Sam, a reminder about your 2:30 p.m. appointment tomorrow at Brook Salon. Need to change it? Tap here and pick another time.
R: Same-day reminder | You have an appointment today. | See you at 2:30 p.m. today. Parking is behind the shop. Running late? Let us know.
R: Confirm request | Reply YES. | Please reply YES to confirm your 2:30 p.m. appointment tomorrow, or tap here to move it.
R: Follow-up | Thank you. | Thanks for visiting, Sam. Ready to book your next one? Here is a link.
UL:
- Use the customer's first name and your business name.
- State the time, the date and the place.
- Always include an easy way to change or cancel.
- Keep it human, not corporate.
- Avoid all capitals and excessive punctuation, which look like spam.
H2[consent-and-good-practice]: Consent and good practice
UL:
- Ask for consent to send text messages when you collect the phone number, and say what you will send.
- Keep a record of consent.
- Include a way to opt out.
- Do not mix reminders with promotions without proper permission.
- Send at reasonable hours.
- Use your business name so customers recognise it.
P: If you serve customers in several countries, rules differ. Check the requirements for the places you operate, and ask your provider what they handle.

A link that opens a page to choose another time, or a reply in the chat, turns a likely no-show into a rebooking and frees the slot early. TABLE:

H2[how-to-test-what-works-for-you]: How to test what works for you
OL:
- Record your current no-show rate for a month.
- Change one thing at a time: add an SMS reminder, change the timing or add a change link.
- Compare the no-show and rescheduling rates after a month.
- Split by service or customer type to see where it works best.
- Keep what helps and drop what does not.
P: A short test beats a long argument about which channel is best. Read the complete plan in [how to reduce no-shows](/blog/eliminate-service-no-shows-playbook) and see [how an AI assistant supports confirmations and reminders](/blog/ai-appointment-booking).
H2[common-mistakes]: Common mistakes
UL:
- Sending only one reminder, too early.
- Sending too many reminders, too close together.
- Leaving out the change link.
- Using vague wording without the time or place.
- Forgetting to ask for consent.
- Never checking whether reminders arrive.
H2[worked-example-choosing-a-mix-for-a-clinic]: Worked example: choosing a mix for a clinic
P: A small clinic has 200 appointments a month, a no-show rate of around 12 percent, which is 24 missed slots, and an average appointment value of $90. That is about $2,160 a month lost. The clinic currently sends a single email reminder two days before.
TABLE:
H: Change | Cost | Expected effect (illustrative)
R: Add a same-day text reminder | Small per-message cost | A modest drop in no-shows, especially among people who do not read email quickly
R: Add a one-tap reschedule link to every reminder | Setup time | Turns some would-be no-shows into rebookings
R: Add a confirm-or-release request the day before | Setup time | Frees slots earlier for the waitlist
R: Add a standby list | Setup time | Fills some of the freed slots
P: If these steps together recover even a quarter of the missed slots, that is six appointments a month, roughly $540, for a small monthly cost. The figures are illustrative, but the method works with your own: measure the baseline, change one thing at a time and compare.
H2[reminder-channels-beyond-sms-and-email]: Reminder channels beyond SMS and email
UL:
- **Messaging apps such as WhatsApp:** familiar and two-way, but need setup and approved templates for reminders outside the reply window. See [the WhatsApp Cloud API guide](/blog/whatsapp-cloud-api-integration-guide).
- **App notifications:** free and immediate if customers have your app, but only useful for businesses with one.
- **Phone calls:** high effort but effective for high-value or older customers who prefer talking.
- **Calendar invitations:** an invite in the customer's own calendar is a reminder they set up themselves.
P: Use the channels your customers already use. Ask them at booking how they prefer to be reminded, and respect that choice.
H2[keep-records-of-what-you-sent]: Keep records of what you sent
P: Log every reminder: who received it, when, through which channel and whether the customer replied or changed the booking. When a customer says they never got a message, you can check. The same log shows which reminder types get replies, so you can drop the ones that do nothing and strengthen the ones that work.
H2[time-zones-language-and-accessibility]: Time zones, language and accessibility
P: State the time zone if customers may be elsewhere, write reminders in the customer's language where you can, and keep the text plain and readable for people using screen readers or large fonts. Avoid relying on images or unusual characters in messages, which may not display on every phone. Test your reminders on a couple of different devices and email apps before you rely on them, and check what a customer sees when they tap the change link on a small screen.
H2[what-to-do-when-a-reminder-goes-unanswered]: What to do when a reminder goes unanswered
P: If you asked for confirmation and did not get it, the day before is the moment to act. Send one more short message, and if the appointment is high value or the customer has missed before, make a quick call. If the slot cannot be confirmed, offer it to your standby list. Be kind and consistent: you are protecting your time, not punishing the customer.
H2[frequently-asked-questions]: Frequently asked questions
FAQ:
Q: Are text messages better than email for appointment reminders?
A: Text messages are noticed faster, so they suit last-minute reminders. Email holds more detail. Most businesses use both.
Q: How many reminders should I send?
A: Usually two or three: a confirmation at booking, one a day or two before and a short one a few hours before.
Q: When should I send appointment reminders?
A: A common pattern is at booking, two to three days before, the day before and a few hours before. Adjust for how far ahead people book.
Q: Do I need consent to text customers?
A: Often yes. Rules vary by country, so check the requirements, ask for consent when you collect the number and keep a record.
Q: What should a reminder include?
A: The date, time, place, your business name and a way to change or cancel. Add preparation notes in email.
