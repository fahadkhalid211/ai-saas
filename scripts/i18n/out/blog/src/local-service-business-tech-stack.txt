P: Small business owners are bombarded with software. Every week brings a new tool that promises to save time, win customers or organise everything. The result is often a pile of overlapping subscriptions, half-set-up accounts and a phone full of apps nobody opens. A local service business, whether a salon, clinic, trade, studio or consultancy, needs remarkably little to run well: a way to be found, a way to be booked, a way to be paid, a way to keep the books, a way to talk to customers and a way to stay safe. This guide sets out a sensible stack in order of priority, with what each layer must do, what to look for, what to skip until you need it and how to keep the total small and manageable. It is not a list of brands, since the right product depends on your country and budget, but a set of criteria you can apply, with pointers to guides on the harder decisions.
H2[principles-for-a-small-stack]: Principles for a small stack
UL:
- **Solve problems, not gaps in a feature list.** Add a tool only when a specific problem justifies it.
- **Prefer fewer tools that do the job over many that overlap.**
- **Check that data can be exported** so you are never trapped.
- **Choose tools your team will actually use;** simplicity beats power.
- **Budget total cost,** including time to set up and learn, not just the subscription.
- **Review annually** and cancel what you do not use.

H2[the-stack-in-priority-order]: The stack in priority order
TABLE:
H: Layer | Job | Must have | Skip for now
R: 1. Website | Be found, explain what you do, show prices and how to book | Mobile-friendly, fast, clear services and contact, your hours and location | Complex e-commerce, animated designs
R: 2. Google Business Profile and directories | Appear in local searches and maps | Accurate name, address, phone, hours and photos | Dozens of minor directories
R: 3. Booking | Let customers book without phoning | Clear slots, confirmations, reminders, ability to change | Complex resource scheduling if you do not need it
R: 4. Payments | Take money simply | A trusted card and payment provider, receipts | Multiple providers
R: 5. Accounting | Track income, expenses and tax | Invoicing, bank feeds or easy imports, reports for your accountant | Advanced forecasting
R: 6. Email and calendar | Communicate professionally | A business email on your own domain, shared calendar | Multiple mailboxes per person
R: 7. Customer chat | Answer questions and capture enquiries at any hour | Answers from your own content, lead capture, handover | Complex automation
R: 8. Reviews and reputation | Collect and display feedback | A simple way to request reviews and reply to them | Expensive reputation suites
R: 9. Security and backups | Protect accounts and data | A password manager, two-factor sign-in, backups | Enterprise security tools
P: If you are just starting, layers one to five are the essentials. The rest are worth adding as you grow.
H2[layer-notes]: Layer notes
H2[website-and-local-search]: Website and local search
P: Your website is your shop window and your answer sheet. Put prices or ranges, services, hours, location, how to book and real photos on it. Keep your Google Business Profile up to date, since many local customers find you there first. A website that answers questions also gives a chat assistant good material to use; see [importing your website into a chatbot](/blog/dynamic-web-scraping-sitemap-ingestion-rag).
H2[booking]: Booking
P: Online booking removes phone tag and captures customers who would not call. The important features are showing real availability, confirming instantly, sending reminders and allowing changes without a phone call. Crescelo's built-in booking offers a slot picker based on opening hours and one slot length, confirmation emails with a private change link and reminders; for per-service durations or buffers, use a scheduling tool such as Calendly or Cal.com, which the assistant can link to.
H2[payments-and-accounting]: Payments and accounting
P: Choose a well-known payment provider available in your country and an accounting tool your accountant is happy with. Connect them so income flows in without retyping. Do not choose accounting software by features alone: ask your accountant which they prefer, because it saves you both time.
H2[email-calendar-and-chat]: Email, calendar and chat
P: Use email on your own domain and set up SPF, DKIM and DMARC so your messages arrive; see [how to set up SPF, DKIM and DMARC](/blog/how-to-set-up-spf-dkim-dmarc-business-email). A customer chat assistant answers the routine questions, books appointments and captures leads, out of hours as well as during them, and hands over to you when a person is needed. Look for answers from your own content, a clear fallback when it does not know, lead alerts by email or Slack and honest pricing. See [chat analytics for service businesses](/blog/service-business-chat-analytics-playbook).
H2[reviews]: Reviews
P: Ask every customer for a review with a direct link, and reply to all of them. See [asking for reviews without being pushy](/blog/collect-reviews-ratings-automated-chat).
H2[security-and-backups]: Security and backups
P: A password manager, two-factor sign-in on every important account and a tested backup are the most valuable and cheapest protections. See [securing your chatbot account](/blog/setup-rbac-2fa-team-security) and [how to back up business data](/blog/how-to-back-up-business-data-3-2-1-rule).

H2[how-the-tools-should-fit-together]: How the tools should fit together
UL:
- Your website links to booking, and the chat assistant offers booking in the conversation.
- Leads from chat go to email, Slack or a spreadsheet you actually check; see [lead routing](/blog/instant-lead-routing-chat).
- Booking confirmations go from your own domain.
- Payments and accounting connect.
- Reviews are requested from the same follow-up routine as rebooking; see [follow-up after the appointment](/blog/post-appointment-nurture-rebooking-sequences).
P: Draw the flow once on paper. If something depends on a person copying data between tools every day, look for a simpler connection.
H2[yearly-review]: Yearly review
OL:
- List every tool and its cost.
- Mark each as used daily, weekly or never.
- Cancel the ones you never use.
- Check that data exports still work.
- Note pain points and search for a single tool that could replace two.
- Review access and roles for each tool.
H2[common-mistakes]: Common mistakes
UL:
- Buying tools before defining problems.
- Overlapping subscriptions.
- No export path.
- Forgetting security and backups.
- Choosing by feature count.
- Never reviewing.
H2[frequently-asked-questions]: Frequently asked questions
FAQ:
Q: What software does a local service business need?
A: A website, a local search profile, booking, payments, accounting, business email, a customer chat assistant, a review process and basic security and backups.
Q: What should I buy first?
A: The website, local profile, booking, payments and accounting, then add the rest as needed.
Q: Do I need a chatbot?
A: It helps if you receive repeated questions or enquiries outside opening hours. Try one on your own content first.
Q: How do I avoid overspending?
A: Add tools only for specific problems, prefer tools that do several jobs and review subscriptions yearly.
Q: How should the tools connect?
A: Website to booking, chat to booking and leads, payments to accounting, all using your own email domain.
Q: How often should I review the stack?
A: Once a year, and whenever a tool stops earning its cost.
